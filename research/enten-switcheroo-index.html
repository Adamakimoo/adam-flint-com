How this was built — and what it doesn't cover

Sources. Every entry marked transcript was read from CNN's own published transcript at transcripts.cnn.com, which is the primary record. The speaker label was checked each time, so anchors' uses of the word (John Berman, Boris Sanchez and a show tease all used it in this period) are excluded — this index is Enten only.

What "verified" means here. CNN publishes these as rush transcripts, so wording can be lightly off from the broadcast and punctuation is inconsistent. Quotes are reproduced as CNN printed them. Three entries are marked index snippet: the phrase appears in the search index for that transcript page, but the extraction pass couldn't re-confirm the sentence in the page body, so treat the wording as close-but-unconfirmed.

Video. CNN does not publish a clip for most of these, and clips can't be redistributed, so links point at the original source that hosts the video. Where I could tie a specific clip to a specific segment, there's a watch button; where I couldn't, the transcript link is the record. Two of the clips are HuffPost's unlisted YouTube uploads — they play from the direct link but won't turn up in a YouTube search.

Known gaps. This is thorough, not exhaustive, and I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise:

  • CNN's transcript archive doesn't cover every hour of every show, and search indexing of it is incomplete — segments that aired but were never transcribed or indexed can't be found this way.
  • Enten also appears on CNN podcasts, digital video and Enten roundup compilations that aren't in the TV transcript archive at all.
  • Two further segments are well documented by reporters but I could not locate their transcripts, so they sit at the bottom of the index marked secondary source rather than being dropped or quietly upgraded.
  • He uses the word roughly weekly, so this index goes stale fast. The newest entry here is 10 September 2026.
